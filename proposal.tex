\section{Introduction}

Mechanistic interpretation is the discovery of relationships between molecular entities such as genes, proteins, and RNA. Systems biology approaches this by studying the interactions between those entities rather than their individual properties~\cite{hartwell1999molecular, barabasi2004network}, seeking to identify \textit{modules}, groups of functionally related entities \comment{entities appears 3 times}, and to characterize the mechanisms underlying their behavior. Because these interactions span multiple scales and regulatory layers, modern analysis relies on multiplex representations that include the same genes across several layers with different edge types~\cite{barabasi2011network, mucha2010community, de2013mathematical}, over which propagation methods such as random walk with restart (RWR) identify functionally related neighborhoods~\cite{kohler2008walking, cowen2017network}. Module-level analysis has become foundational for identifying disease mechanisms, prioritizing drug targets, and interpreting polygenic phenotypes~\cite{menche2015uncovering}. Methods such as iterative random forest (iRF)~\cite{basu2018iterative, cliff2019high} have accelerated mechanistic interpretation by constructing biologically informed networks that scientists explore to generate hypotheses.

As these high-throughput and algorithmic methods increase the resolution and scope of networks, biological interpretation has become increasingly mediated by large, multi-scale graph structures. To comprehensively examine these large networks and supporting databases, mechanistic exploration requires iterative querying and multi-step reasoning. Although machine learning and AI methods such as \textsc{Mentor}~\cite{mentor} have refined mechanistic focus into groups of functionally related modules of genes, these modules can still be massive and intractable for individual researchers to systematically explore. Additionally, most research emphasizes a deep-but-narrow focus on a small subset of functionally related genes, which can overlook broader, yet biologically meaningful, network context, particularly in highly interactive systems such as living organisms. As biological networks grow in complexity, the combinatorial space of interactions expands rapidly, while human interpretive capacity remains fixed.

While large language models (LLMs) can synthesize large volumes of information, frontier inference cost scales with exploration length and reasoning depth, limiting deployment for long-horizon network traversal. This mismatch creates a scaling limit in biological mechanistic interpretation: rich relational structures can be computed, but downstream synthesis remains constrained by human bandwidth and model cost. Consequently, the speed, breadth, and contextual depth of mechanistic discovery are limited, particularly for complex traits, polygenic diseases, and multi-layer regulatory systems.

Open-source LLMs present an opportunity to aid scientists in mechanistic discovery tasks without being cost prohibitive. Language models reason more reliably when prompted to produce intermediate steps~\cite{wei2022chain, kojima2022large}, and equipping them with external tools supplies a layer of deterministic verification that reduces hallucination and preserves provenance~\cite{schick2023toolformer, yao2022react}. Adding an explicit verification stage improves factual reliability further, whether through iterative self-critique without weight updates~\cite{madaan2023selfrefine, shinn2023reflexion} or through a dedicated verifier with separate weights~\cite{eubiota}. Applied to gene set interpretation, these ideas have produced language-model embeddings of gene function~\cite{chen2025simple}, database-backed self-verifying pipelines~\cite{wang2025geneagent}, and multi-agent systems that synthesize mechanistic narratives~\cite{mentor-ia, eubiota}. However, these pipelines depend on frontier model inference, and none are trained end-to-end as a policy over verifiable rewards.

Reinforcement learning supplies the machinery to close that gap. Preference optimization techniques, such as direct preference optimization (DPO), align a policy to pairwise judgments~\cite{christiano2017rlhf, ouyang2022instructgpt, dpo}, while policy gradient methods, such as proximal policy optimization (PPO) and group relative policy optimization (GRPO) optimize against scalar rewards over complete rollouts~\cite{schulman2017proximal, shao2024deepseekmath}. Verifiable rewards have been shown to elicit long-horizon reasoning behavior at frontier scale~\cite{guo2025deepseek}. These methods have been applied to tool-using agents for web browsing, API handling, and multi-step web tasks~\cite{nakano2021webgpt, qin2023toolllm, putta2024agent}, but not to a policy whose rewards are grounded in network-derived biological targets.

These current systems share a limitation: they treat networks as a static object to query and retrieve, rather than learning the general rules it obeys. Nothing carries over to a network the system was not handed, so every new cell type, tissue, or disease has no prior representation. Because biological structure is itself context-specific, this is a large portion of the problem space. What is needed instead is a \textit{world model} which internalizes multiplex structure and reasons mechanistically from it.

Sequence models trained on synthetic tasks form internal, causal models of the process behind their data, and those models generalize to states never seen during training~\cite{li2023othello}. This works because the rules that generate the data are compact and recur across many examples, while the specific data does not. Two consequences shape this proposal. First, a model can learn the operators that regenerate structure, but it cannot memorize irreducible content that follows no compact rule, so biological edge weights, walk scores, and distances belong in a deterministic runtime rather than in the weights. Second, a world model emerges only under dense sampling of the generation process, which a single network queried with fixed templates cannot supply.

We therefore propose \textsc{Mentor-RL}, an open-weight model trained on a structured lattice of multiplex networks. Every network in the lattice is built by the same rule: the predictive expression network (PEN) layers selected for a biological context define its gene universe, the remaining layers are induced on that universe, and all layers are stacked. Varying the context, whether a cell type, a tissue, a brain region, or an organ system, varies the resulting network while holding the construction rule fixed. We call this population the \textit{multiplex lattice}, and it supplies the dense sampling that a single network cannot. The invariant we train on is the operator that maps a multiplex to its structure, together with the way layer composition drives it. We expect hierarchies to differ strongly across contexts, with distinct clades emerging in different cell types and regions. This divergence is biological signal rather than artifact: a model that has learned the operator will produce correspondingly different hierarchies for different contexts, and recovering the divergence correctly is a stronger result than reproducing conservation.

A hierarchy alone cannot serve as the world model, because it is a lossy abstraction of the multiplex it summarizes. Two clades that sit far apart in a tree may still be joined by short, biologically supported paths through bridge nodes in the underlying network, which is why a signal such as drug-target enrichment can appear in several distant branches and yet act through a single connected system. The model must hold the network itself and reason about the cross-clade connectivity that the tree suppresses. We use the hierarchy to find and prioritize candidates, and the network to confirm them.

This proposal pursues four objectives. First, we define the multiplex lattice and ground it in a standard biological ontology, so that every network carries a canonical address. Second, we design a supervised curriculum that trains on multiplex operators, holding three structural views of the lattice as co-equal targets and admitting a training example only when its target passes a typed validator against deterministic runtime output. Third, we use the resulting checkpoint to train a tool-using agent, formalized as a sequential decision process over a structured state and scored by deterministic, schema-grounded rewards. Fourth, we make the world-model claim falsifiable through two flagship evaluations: generating a hierarchy for a context never seen in training, and projecting a drug target onto the hierarchy before returning to the network to recover the connectivity that justifies it, supported by activation probes that test whether the recovered structure is causal. Together these aim to make mechanistic discovery broader, faster, and more context-aware.

\section{Proposed Methods}
\subsection{Multiplex Lattice}\label{sec:ch4-lattice}
In this section, we define the multiplex lattice that is the substrate for our mechanistic world model. The lattice is constructed from a set of multiplex networks, where entities such as genes and proteins are represented as nodes, and their interactions within a context are represented as edges. We select PEN layers for a context to define its \textit{gene universe}, or the set of genes that the network operates over. The remaining, non-PEN layers are then \textit{induced} on that universe, restricted to the genes present in the PEN layers and the edges among them. Finally, all layers in the context are \textit{stacked} to form a multiplex network that captures the multi-layered interactions among genes in that context. This process means that there is no single shared backbone across the lattice; even layers drawn from a common source become context-specific once they are induced. Two sampled points in the lattice may contain different nodes and edges, and therefore different hierarchies. 

The lattice comprises a tiered structure that captures the relationships between different contexts. We visualize an example brain multiplex lattice in Figure~\ref{fig:lattice}. Its most granular component is a single cell type, such as an excitatory neuron, inhibitory neuron, or glial cell, within one tissue of one region. Combining cell types yields a tissue-level multiplex, combining tissues yields a region-level multiplex such as the prefrontal cortex, hippocampus, or cerebellum, and combining regions builds up to the whole-brain multiplex we already hold \comment{haven't specified this multiplex yet; do we need background or a better introduction?}. Every tier is therefore a multiplex in its own right, built by the same rule, rather than a label over the tier below. Disease networks for anxiety, suicide, and pan-substance-use-disorder are points in this same lattice, which makes transfer meaningful: a model that has learned the construction operator can be applied to them directly.

Higher tiers are built by applying the same rule to a union of contexts. We take the union of the parts' gene universes, re-induce the non-PEN layers on it, and stack the parts' PEN layers on top. This process makes lattice sampling highly combinatorial, as changing a single PEN layer changes two things at once. The gene universe grows or shrinks, and the backbone is re-induced to match. A pair of multiplexes that differ by one layer therefore also differ by the backbone that layer brought with it. Measuring the effect of a layer on its own requires holding the gene universe fixed, which we do via controlled ablation. The lattice also holds far more combinations than we can build, so tier counts and sampling rates stay provisional until divergence validation shows how much contexts actually differ.

\subsection{Context Ontology}
In order to train \textsc{Mentor-RL} in a reproducible way, we define a \textit{context ontology} that specifies the relationships between different contexts. This allows us to name, version, and match the lattice against external data. We use several grounded ontologies to define the context hierarchy. Anatomy is defined by UBERON~\cite{uberon}, while cell types are defined by the Cell Ontology~\cite{cellontology}, extended by the Provisional Cell Ontology~\cite{pcl} for transcriptomic brain types that the Cell Ontology does not yet hold. Developmental stages are defined by HsapDv \comment{need citation}, anatomy to cell type mappings are defined by the Human Reference Atlas~\cite{asctb}, and node metadata follows the CELLxGENE schema~\cite{cellxgene}. Because the Human Reference Atlas is a whole-body construction, this paradigm is extensible past the brain, allowing future scaling.

It is important to note that the context ontology defines a partial order, not a tree, over contexts. Because some cell types are present in multiple tissues, and some tissues are present in multiple regions, strict hierarchies are not possible \comment{this seems to contradict the introduction of the lattice in the previous section}. Instead, we define a \textit{join} operation over the ontology: the join of two contexts is the smallest context that contains both of them. The join is also what ties the ontology to the construction rule. Joining two contexts names exactly the union multiplex described in Section~\ref{sec:ch4-lattice}. The ontology gives the address, and the construction rule builds the network.

The model reads the ontology as supplied input, and it is never stored in the weights. Rather than memorizing the ontology, the model learns two separate things. First, it learns to navigate the ontology, returning a context's parents, children, and siblings, the join of two contexts, and the distance between them. It also learns the construction rule that turns a context into a multiplex. The ontology tells the model where it is, and the construction rule tells it what to build there. Because only the terms change, the same navigation transfers when the lattice extends to other organ systems.

\subsection{Three Structural Views}\label{sec:ch4-views}
In order to represent this ontology-based lattice structure as a training target, we define three equally weighted structural views of a multiplex network. These are bottom-up random-walk-with-restart lines-of-evidence (RWR-LOE) neighborhoods, top-down \textsc{Mentor-EV}-derived hierarchies, and network connectivity. Each view is a different representation of the same underlying multiplex with its own biases.

The RWR-LOE view is a bottom-up representation of a multiplex that captures the local topology around each node~\cite{kainer2025rwrtoolkit}. For each node in a given multiplex, we perform a random walk with restart over all layers of the multiplex, treating each layer as an independent line of evidence. The walk returns a probability distribution over all nodes in the network, with higher probabilities representing closer proximity to the seed. We then threshold the distribution using a geometric elbow, which cuts the score-versus-rank curve at its bend. The genes above the cutoff form a module of genes that are proximal to the seed over multiple lines of evidence. From RWR-LOE modules, we can learn several operator types, including rank and score lookup, rank comparison, and relations between modules. \comment{RWR-LOE figure?}

\textsc{Mentor-EV}-derived modules represent an alternative, top-down view of the multiplex. \textsc{Mentor-EV} applies \textsc{Mentor}~\cite{mentor} using every gene in the multiplex as a seed, so the resulting hierarchy spans the full gene universe rather than a supplied gene set. To create \textsc{Mentor-EV} modules, we first use RWR to compute the probability distribution for each node in the multiplex. Then we use Spearman correlation to compute the pairwise similarity between the RWR vectors of each pair of nodes. We subtract those correlations from one to create dissimilarity scores, which we then use to build a dissimilarity matrix over all nodes in the multiplex. Finally, we use hierarchical clustering to create a dendrogram, in which leaves are genes and each internal node defines a \textit{clade}, the set of genes beneath it. Cutting the dendrogram at a given merge height produces a family of nested modules and controls their granularity. From \textsc{Mentor-EV} modules, we can learn several operator types, including module membership, module comparison, least common ancestors, and distance between modules. \comment{MENTOR-EV figure?}

Every one of these targets is read from a single dendrogram. Because we never compare one tree against another, we do not need two hierarchies to agree. This is what makes the divergence described in Section~\ref{sec:ch4-lattice} trainable rather than an obstacle: hierarchies differ across contexts, but each one supplies its own targets.

However, RWR-LOE and \textsc{Mentor-EV} modules are both lossy abstractions of the underlying multiplex structure. Two clades on distant branches of the dendrogram may still be connected by short paths through a small number of bridge nodes, and a neighborhood cut at the elbow drops genes that remain reachable in a few hops. As such, we also include learnable network connectivity operators as a third view, which include cross-clade path recovery, connector and bridge node identification, path decomposition by supporting layer, and cohesion tested against a null. These operators allow the model to reason about the underlying multiplex structure and recover connectivity that is not captured by the other two views.

\subsection{Stage 1: Multiplex World Model}
We propose a two-stage training pipeline for \textsc{Mentor-RL}. In the first stage, we train a world model to internalize the multiplex lattice by learning the operators that generate its structure. We outline the framework for Stage 1 in this section. Section~\ref{sec:ch4-runtime} describes the deterministic runtime and tools that are used to generate targets. Section~\ref{sec:ch4-curriculum} covers the supervised curriculum that progressively teaches the model to navigate the lattice. Section~\ref{sec:ch4-corpus} describes how corpora are generated and validated for each curriculum stage, and Section~\ref{sec:ch4-splits} details the train, validation, and test splits across contexts.

\subsubsection{Deterministic Runtime and Tool Contract}\label{sec:ch4-runtime}
To interface the lattice environment with the training pipeline, we use a compiled native multiplex library exposed to Python through \texttt{ctypes} bindings~\cite{cpp}. Corpus construction, training, and optimization code are implemented in Python~\cite{python}, while graph storage and graph operations are handled by the native runtime for efficient execution on large multiplexes. Multiplex networks are persisted as raw, CSR-style binary stores described by JSON manifests; all graph input and output is binary, with the manifest carrying the layer names, node, and build metadata needed to load the graph.

The runtime uses a set of optimized multiplex functions developed as part of the \textsc{Mentor}~\cite{mentor} and \textsc{Mentor-IA}~\cite{mentor-ia} projects, known as RWR++. Core operators include neighborhood retrieval, shortest-path search, random walk with restart on both monoplex and multiplex views, and subgraph induction. These operators provide the primary graph-facing actions available to the model.

We define several rules to structure our graph tools as learnable and interpretable operators. First, each tool only takes biologically meaningful arguments, such as gene identifiers, layer names, and module identifiers. This way, when learning to produce a tool call, the model is reasoning about the biology rather than the file system or command-line interface. Second, we defer to the runtime for numeric computations. The tokenization process makes it difficult for the model to learn to produce precise floating-point numbers, so we instead have the model call a tool to compute any statistic it needs. Third, we enforce determinism, ensuring that the same arguments, graph version, layer scope, and parameter version always return identical output. This allows for caching of tool calls and reproducible scoring.

All runtime operators return both graph objects and provenance metadata. In addition to nodes, edges, and scores, each returned object records the relevant layer identity, operator parameters, and source information needed to trace the final interpretation back to the graph evidence used to construct it. To ensure consistency across serialized multiplex files, C++ runtime objects, Python wrappers, and logged training artifacts, all nodes are represented in a single canonical identifier space and all edges are referenced using deterministic, layer-aware identifiers. We list all runtime tools in Table~\ref{tab:ch4-tools}.

\subsubsection{Supervised Curriculum}\label{sec:ch4-curriculum}
In order to train the model to internalize the multiplex lattice, we design a supervised curriculum that progressively teaches the model to navigate the lattice and learn operators that generate its structure. We define two rules to govern the structure of the curriculum. First, we use closed-book training only on targets that are bounded, stable conventions. Identifiers such as layer tags, layer families, module identifier formats, and direction conventions are small, finite, and do not change, so memorizing them through training is acceptable. Second, open-book training means reading, not computing. We read unbounded and unstable outputs, such as continuous values and statistics, from the runtime rather than memorizing them. Later in the curriculum, rule two becomes extended to tool-call training, where the model learns to parse returned objects instead of restating these objects from memory. This distinction between what the model can memorize and what it must compute is important for generalization: the model should learn the operators that generate structure, not the specific values that result from them.

Using these rules, we define a nine-step supervised training curriculum that gradually introduces more complex operators and tasks. Each stage builds on the previous one, starting with basic graph navigation and moving towards more complex tasks such as module comparison, path recovery, and enrichment analysis. For brevity, we group similar curriculum stages together when describing them. However, a full curriculum table is provided in Table~\ref{tab:ch4-curriculum}.

Curriculum stages S0-S3 focus on foundational graph navigation operators. First, S0 learns ensembl-to-symbol and symbol-to-ensembl conversions, which are necessary for all downstream tasks. S1 introduces the lattice coordinate system, teaching the model to navigate the context ontology, retrieve layer information, and understand the meanings behind rankings and metrics. S2 trains on atomic graph facts and their negative examples, such as edge existence, neighbors, layer membership, and degree. S3 introduces path-based operators and cross-layer operators, and it teaches connectivity by training on connector and bridge nodes.

After foundational training is complete, stages S4-S6 focus on the three structural views of the multiplex. S4 teaches the model to operate on bottom-up RWR-LOE neighborhoods, S5 trains the model for top-down hierarchical modules, and S6 focuses on unifying these views through network connectivity. Additionally, S5 includes training examples that teach correspondence between the RWR-LOE and \textsc{Mentor-EV} views, which keeps them from being learned as unrelated skills.

Stages S7 and S8 build learned lattice structure concepts by adding projection and enrichment tasks. S7 teaches gene set projection through the composition of RWR-LOE and \textsc{Mentor-EV} operators; first, RWR-LOE is performed on a target gene set, and then the resulting neighborhood is enriched against the \textsc{Mentor-EV} module hierarchy at every level. The projection process yields an enrichment score and significance value for every clade at every level, measuring how much of the target's neighborhood falls within it. High enrichment across several distant clades is evidence of a single connected system, rather than unrelated biological processes. This reinforces the idea that tree distance does not mean that clades are unrelated (Section~\ref{sec:ch4-views}). Therefore, the recovery of bridge nodes and short cross-clade paths, along with the layers supporting them, is essential. S8 teaches composition: the model learns to predict how neighborhoods, clades, and connectivity change when a layer is added or removed, which is crucial for understanding the contextual differences across sampled lattice points.  

Finally, S9 consolidates the curriculum by combining previous stages and trains the checkpoint for the multi-step reasoning required in Stage 2. The model is trained on tool-call construction, selection, parsing, provenance, and task refusal, all of which are crucial for the mechanistic agent (Stage 2, Section~\ref{sec:ch4-agent-loop}) to operate effectively in the multiplex lattice. 

The curriculum structure allows us to scale difficulty along several axes during training. We scale lattice tier, starting with single cell types and moving to tissues, regions, and eventually the entire brain. Cut-height granularity defines the size of modules, starting with coarse views of clades and moving to finer-grained views. We also scale operator complexity and book mode explicitly through our curriculum design. To measure progress along these axes, we define gates that the model must pass to move on to the next curriculum stage. All gates utilize held-out question phrasings and held-out graph regions per family, while also including negative examples to measure abstention. These gates are task-specific, ensuring that the model has correctly learned the operators from each curriculum stage before moving on to the next.

\subsubsection{Corpus Generation and Validation}\label{sec:ch4-corpus}
To build the training corpora for each curriculum stage, we generate questions procedurally from various points on the lattice. By sampling many contexts and layers, we allow the model to see each operator applied to many different networks, which encourages generalization. All targets have their ground truth produced by the deterministic runtime defined in Section~\ref{sec:ch4-runtime}, and examples are cached with their layer descriptor, metric, seed set, and graph version to ensure reproducibility. Rather than generating corpora for all curriculum stages at once, we generate them on demand for each stage, allowing us to adjust sampling strategy and training splits based on prior stage performance. 

In order to validate our corpus, we check every answer field against a typed validator, and each question family composes the validators its fields require. A path field, for example, is accepted only when the node sequence is a valid walk whose length matches the runtime shortest path, so any optimal path is accepted rather than a single reference answer. A claim field is accepted only when the stated claim appears in the enumerated allowed set and no disallowed claim is asserted, which is the mechanism behind the calibration negatives in S2. Validation is all-or-nothing: an example enters the corpus only if every required field passes. In addition to admitting training data, these validators are used at evaluation time to score model outputs, ensuring that answers meet the criteria defined by the curriculum. This validation process gives us deterministic ground truth for training and evaluation, allowing us to measure the model's performance accurately and consistently across contexts and curriculum stages.

In order for the model to effectively learn, we must generate a large dataset to provide sufficient signal. Our provisional lattice corpus will include 24 separate contexts alongside a full brain multiplex. Formatting questions from these contexts, we expect to generate approximately 3 million supervised examples, with roughly 10\% closed-book, 75\% open-book, and 15\% tool-call examples. From these 3 million examples, we expect about 1.2 billion training tokens. Storage is dominated by the cached ground truth rather than the text, at an estimated 2 to 4 terabytes across the 24 contexts, which is the second reason to materialize corpora in curriculum order rather than all at once. However, these numbers are provisional and will be adjusted based on the training results at each curriculum stage.

\subsubsection{Splits and Leakage Control}\label{sec:ch4-splits}
In order to truly evaluate generalization capability, we must thoroughly control for leakage between training, validation, and test splits. While each context contains a separate network, these networks still share genes, and therefore share some of the underlying edges among those genes. To control for leakage, we define three levels of hold-out validation: context, clade, and tuple. To hold out a context, we partition lattice points into training, validation, and test sets, ensuring that they are never split internally by gene. We choose two to four contexts to hold out entirely from our 24-context lattice, so these hierarchies are never seen during training. 

In addition to contexts, we hold out clades by selecting maximal subtrees at a chosen anchor height. This is necessary because a gene appears in every clade along its hierarchical ancestry; therefore, splitting by individual clades could leak membership information due to the dendrogram's nested structure.

Finally, we partition specific query units into separate splits. A query unit is a seed with its target set, together with the cut height for hierarchical modules. We validate these test tuples by checking their Jaccard overlap with training tuples. A test tuple is permissible only if its target set has a Jaccard overlap below a provisional 0.2 with any training tuple sharing the same seed. Two further controls apply at this level. We cap how many high-degree seeds enter any single split, because RWR-LOE neighborhoods around hub genes overlap heavily even when contexts and clades are cleanly separated. We also keep both members of an S8 minimal pair on the same side of the split, so that a held-out multiplex is never trivially inferable from a partner that differs from it by a single PEN layer.

In order to ensure compliance with this leakage control, we audit the training, validation, and test splits for each curriculum stage. We check gene overlap between train and test sets, the fraction of test clades whose ancestry appears in train, the representation of hub nodes in the data, and the count of minimal pairs split across sides, which should be zero. If any of these checks fail, then splits are readjusted and corpora are regenerated. One source of leakage remains after these controls. Because contexts share genes, they also share the backbone edges among those shared genes, so this component is bounded and reported rather than removed. We accept it because what is invariant across the lattice is the construction operator rather than any particular backbone. This process ensures that the model is evaluated on truly unseen data, and the extent of any leakage is known and stored prior to evaluation.

The agent is trained using reinforcement learning over long-horizon mechanistic reasoning tasks. We formalize the agent loop and structured state in Section~\ref{sec:ch4-agent-loop}, and we describe the task cases, corpora, and reward design in Sections~\ref{sec:ch4-task-cases}--\ref{sec:ch4-reward}. We model mechanistic exploration as a sequential decision process executed by a single policy with an internal self-verification step. The loop begins when the user provides a query $q$ and optional evidence $e$, which may include free text, seed genes, or a multiplex. These initialize the working interpretation $I_0$ and the structured state $S_0$.

At each step the policy first acts. Given the current decision context, it emits a textual reasoning step $r_t$ naming its current subgoal, together with an optional tool call $a_t$. A tool call is executed by the deterministic runtime $\varepsilon$ of Section~\ref{sec:ch4-runtime} to produce an observation $o_t$; when no call is made, the observation is empty. The same policy then verifies. Conditioned on the decision context and on its own reasoning, action, and observation, it updates the interpretation and state and emits a continuation decision $z_t$ of \texttt{continue}, \texttt{revise}, or \texttt{stop}. A decision of \texttt{revise} means the next iteration corrects the interpretation and state rather than extending them. One iteration is therefore
\[
r_t, a_t \sim \pi_{\theta}^{\mathrm{act}}(\cdot \mid D_t), \qquad
o_t = \varepsilon(a_t), \qquad
I_{t+1}, S_{t+1}, z_t \sim \pi_{\theta}^{\mathrm{verify}}(\cdot \mid r_t, a_t, o_t, D_t),
\]
where $D_t = (q, e, I_t, S_t)$ is the decision context and $\varepsilon(\varnothing) = \varnothing$ by convention. The actor and verifier are two prompting modes of the same weights, so biological vocabulary and tool-use competence learned in one mode transfer to the other. We treat the risk that the verifier collapses into agreement with the actor as a monitoring concern during training. The loop repeats until the policy emits \texttt{stop} or the decision budget $T_{\max}$ is exhausted, and returns the pair $(I_T, S_T)$: a human-readable answer grounded in the accumulated evidence, and the machine-readable record used for scoring and provenance. \comment{add figure showing the agent loop (better version of the one in main.tex)}

The working interpretation holds the current mechanistic claim, the main evidence supporting that claim, any uncertainty, conflict, or alternative explanation, and the next subgoal the model is trying to resolve. The structured state stores the same hypothesis in a machine-readable form suitable for tool execution, reward computation, and provenance tracking. It contains:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item the user-provided anchors $(q, e)$,
    \item the current relationship status: unknown, partially observed group, validated group, multiple groups, or insufficient support,
    \item the current predicted group or groups,
    \item the evidence and provenance collected so far,
    \item the current predicted mechanistic labels, each tied to the evidence that supports it,
    \item the tool-use counters recording total and invalid calls, and
    \item the remaining budget and continuation state, with the termination signal recorded at the end of the trajectory.
\end{enumerate} \comment{add side-by-side figure showing the working interpretation and structured state}

To ensure that the agent's reasoning is grounded in evidence, we enforce three operating rules. First, every claim must be supported by retrieved evidence, and the agent must state its uncertainty explicitly, or abstain from making a claim when support is weak. Second, the agent must read the \textsc{Mentor-EV} dendrogram modules as an index and confirm its hypotheses on the network, using the same multi-view structure described in Section~\ref{sec:ch4-views}. Finally, the agent must return both a human-readable working interpretation for users and a structured state for record-keeping. These rules are enforced via the reward function and training curriculum, which reinforce these behaviors during the learning process.

\subsubsection{Task Cases and Targets}\label{sec:ch4-task-cases}
We define several tasks and targets for agent training, all of which build on the internalized lattice representation learned in Stage 1. Tasks are divided into three categories: module, connectivity, and projection tasks. For each task, we define a set of targets that we can use to provide deterministic rewards and evaluate the agent's performance.  

Module tasks fall into one of four cases: partial group completion, mechanism explanation, group refinement, and recognition of no shared mechanism. In partial group completion, the agent is given a module with some genes held out and must identify the complete module by adding genes. In mechanism explanation, the agent is given a complete module and must identify the mechanistic evidence that supports it without adding or removing genes. In group refinement, the agent is given a module with extra distractor genes, and it must remove the distractor genes to distill the module into a mechanistically coherent group. Finally, in the recognition of no shared mechanism case, the agent is given a module that is not mechanistically coherent. It must either split the input into several coherent groups, or report that the evidence provides insufficient support for any shared mechanism. \comment{add a figure showing different task type inputs/outputs/ground truth}

Connectivity tasks join the two module views through the network that underlies both. The agent is given two clades that are distant in the \textsc{Mentor-EV} hierarchy and must identify a short path between them, along with the layers that support that path. The agent must also identify any bridge nodes that connect the two modules, and provide mechanistic evidence for the role of those bridge nodes. This task tests the agent's ability to reason about the underlying multiplex structure and recover connectivity that is not captured by the bottom-up or top-down views alone.

Finally, we train the model on projection tasks, which require the agent to project a seed gene's RWR-LOE neighborhood onto the \textsc{Mentor-EV} hierarchy and identify the clades that are enriched for that neighborhood. Because the neighborhood was built by propagation over the network, enrichment landing in several distant clades is evidence of a single connected system rather than unrelated processes, and the agent must read it that way. It must then recover the bridge nodes and short paths that connect those clades and provide mechanistic evidence for their role. Projection tasks are scored on three targets separately: the projection itself, the connectivity recovered to support it, and the interpretation placed on that connectivity. A candidate passes only when all three clear, since a correct projection paired with a failed connectivity recovery is precisely the error that the network view exists to prevent.

The agent curriculum culminates in the applied case, in which the projected seed is a drug target or a disease signal. Here the agent must additionally apply directional and translational filtering. Directional filtering asks whether the candidate intervention opposes the disease-associated direction within a specific module rather than merely acting near it. Translational filtering ranks surviving candidates on evidence strength, adverse-event risk, brain penetration, feasibility, and testability. This case is the repurposing workflow that motivates the disease projections described in Section~\ref{sec:ch4-lattice}. \comment{add a figure showing applied case}

To provide deterministic rewards, we use module membership targets, which are hidden gene sets drawn from bottom-up RWR-LOE neighborhoods and top-down \textsc{Mentor-EV} modules. The agent must identify the correct complete module given either a module with genes held out, a module with extra distractor genes, or a combination of multiple modules. The agent never sees the ground truth modules, so it must determine them based on multi-step reasoning over multiplex structure. Because these modules are derived computationally from network structure, they usually carry no per-entry experimental annotation, so there is no hidden label against which an explanation could be matched. We therefore define mechanistic evidence targets, which score interpretations against the evidence that the model retrieves during exploration. This rewards claims that are grounded in what the agent actually found rather than claims that reproduce an existing annotation, and it leaves room for biology that no database yet records. \comment{this covers the module case, but not projection/connectivity}

\subsubsection{Task Corpora and Trajectory Generation}\label{sec:ch4-corpora}
In order to train the mechanistic agent, we generate a corpus of module tasks drawn from contexts across the lattice. We use two sources of modules: top-down \textsc{Mentor-EV} modules, and bottom-up RWR-LOE neighborhoods. These sources provide complementary views of the multiplex while supplying hidden targets for scoring. Additionally, we generate connectivity and projection tasks from the same lattice points, allowing us to incorporate cross-module structure into the agent's reasoning. 

For each module of size $n$, we deterministically materialize up to four task instances, one per case in Section~\ref{sec:ch4-task-cases}. This ensures that all four task types are represented for as many modules as possible rather than sampled independently. The \texttt{explanation} instance uses the full module as input. The \texttt{recovery} instance drops $k_{\text{drop}}$ genes from the module and the \texttt{refinement} instance adds $k_{\text{add}}$ noise genes, with the full module remaining the hidden target in both cases. The number of genes added or dropped scales with the task difficulty and module size, so that harder instances perturb a larger fraction of the module. The \texttt{none} instance constructs a pseudo-module of unrelated genes as input, with an empty hidden target. 

The two module sources differ only in where distractors come from. For \textsc{Mentor-EV} modules, noise and \texttt{none} genes are drawn from difficulty-modulated \textit{dendrogram distance bands}, defined by walking up the module's ancestry in the tree, so that difficulty controls how topologically close the distractors are to the module. For RWR-LOE modules we substitute rank bands, drawing from ranks just beyond, moderately beyond, or far beyond the seed's elbow cutoff. In both cases \texttt{none} genes are additionally constrained to be conflict-free, so that the sampled genes do not form a coherent module of their own, and all selection is deterministic given a fixed seed, making the corpus reproducible. Module-size bins are assigned after construction from the realized module sizes, and all splits follow the protocol of Section~\ref{sec:ch4-splits}. \comment{maybe add a figure for distractor genes}

For connectivity and projection tasks, we build on previously sampled modules. For connectivity, we sample pairs of clades at a stated tree distance and generate the shortest path between them, along with the layers that support that path. For projection, we sample seed genes and compute their RWR-LOE neighborhoods, and then enrich those neighborhoods against the \textsc{Mentor-EV} hierarchy to identify the clades that are enriched for that neighborhood. The agent must then recover the bridge nodes, paths, and layers that connect those clades, and provide mechanistic evidence for their role. These tasks are scored on three targets separately: the projection itself, the connectivity recovered to support it, and the interpretation placed on that connectivity.

Given a sampled task and initialized state, we sample multiple action candidates $(r_{t,i}, a_{t,i})$ given the current decision context $D_t$ and execute valid tool calls in the deterministic graph runtime. To ensure that candidates with the same prefix explore genuinely distinct regions of the action space, we use verbalized sampling based on the task type. For example, recovery prompts bias candidates towards random-walk expansion, neighborhood expansion, subgraph validation, or a direct decision, while insufficient evidence (\texttt{none}) prompts direct the model towards disconfirming graph evidence. When a sampling round for recovery or refinement tasks fails to produce valid tool calls, we issue a tool-coverage retry that requires a valid graph operator whenever one can be formed, which prevents the branch pool from degenerating into tool-free continuations. Conditioned on each candidate action and observation, the same policy then samples multiple verification candidates, yielding a tree of candidate next states rooted at a shared prefix. The branched sampling scheme is visualized in Figure~\ref{fig:shared-prefix}. \comment{add figure for this}

Each candidate branch is assigned a deterministic local score computed from the structured state, runtime outputs, and hidden module targets. These local scores are used to rank same-prefix candidates and mine DPO preference pairs against that selection. Because several branches frequently tie or fall within a small margin of each other, continuation selection does not reduce to a strict $\arg\max$, but instead, within a tolerance margin $\epsilon$ of the best normalized score, ties are broken with task-specific heuristics such as prompted seed gene retention and tool-supported evidence, pushing the logged trajectory to favor well-grounded candidates over those that score highly by chance. We defer the formal score definitions to Section~\ref{sec:ch4-reward}.

Model prompts carry a compact state rather than a growing transcript. Each prompt supplies the current working interpretation, the relationship status, the predicted groups, the mechanistic labels tied to the evidence handles that support them, the tool and evidence counters, and the current observation. Prior observations are not appended, so prompt length stays bounded as trajectories grow. The full evidence payloads are retained in the logged trajectory artifacts, where they remain available for scoring, audit, preference-pair mining, and expert review.

We provide several query modes and evidence types to simulate the diversity of inputs that an agent might encounter in real-world use cases. During training, we sample queries from a set of task-specific templates that are paraphrased by an open-weight model to ensure linguistic diversity. We provide the agent with either minimal evidence, which includes only the input query and associated seed genes, or graph-supported evidence, which includes a relevant subgraph of the multiplex lattice that is related to the query. At inference time, the agent receives a natural-language query along with optional graph or seed evidence. During trajectory generation, the agent never sees the task type, the true relationship status, or the hidden target. Invalid tool calls during generation return an empty observation and count towards the efficiency penalty, which encourages the agent to learn concise and effective reasoning strategies. 

We define a three-stage curriculum for the mechanistic agent, which gradually increases the complexity of tasks and length of trajectories. The first stage focuses on module tasks, where the agent learns to recover and refine modules from partial or noisy inputs. The second stage introduces connectivity tasks, where the agent must identify paths and bridge nodes between distant clades. The final stage focuses on projection tasks, where the agent must project neighborhoods onto the hierarchy and recover mechanistic evidence for the resulting connections, synthesizing the skills learned in the previous stages. Each stage builds on the previous one, ensuring that the agent develops a comprehensive understanding of the multiplex structure and the mechanistic relationships within it.

\subsubsection{Reward Design}\label{sec:ch4-reward}
We define all training-time scores over structured state transitions, deterministic runtime metadata, and hidden module-derived targets rather than textual reasoning. This design choice keeps scoring reproducible, machine-parseable, and scalable, while avoiding dependence on human judges or semantic evaluation of natural-language trajectories. We decompose all scores into four components: schema compliance, group membership, mechanistic quality, and efficiency. 

Because DPO optimizes over preferred continuations at a single timepoint, we define local branch scoring as 

\begin{equation}\label{eqn:local-reward}
    s^{local}_{t,i,j} = w_s\, s^{schema} + w_c\, \Delta s^{module} + w_m\, \Delta s^{mech} - w_e\, p^{eff} - p^{mismatch}
\end{equation}

where $\Delta s^{mech}$ is the evidence-grounded mechanistic quality delta. Additionally, mechanistic quality is clamped to be non-positive when the candidate degrades the predicted group relative to the task type (e.g. by adding noise genes during recovery or removing true members during refinement), and $p^{mismatch}$ penalizes an \texttt{explanation} candidate that reduces recovery of an already-complete module.

We use $\Delta s_{module}$ to measure the change in module membership accuracy from step $t$ to $t+1$. Because the runtime state may contain multiple predicted groups, we first score a state by the best-matched predicted group:

\begin{equation}\label{eqn:module-state-score}
    s^{module}(z; C, C_{\text{type}}) =
    \max_{G \in \mathcal{G}(z)}
    \left[
        \alpha J(G, C) + \beta P(G, C) + \gamma R(G, C)
    \right],
\end{equation}

where $\mathcal{G}(z)$ denotes the set of predicted groups in state $z$, with an empty group used when no prediction is present. We then define the local module improvement as

\begin{equation}\label{eqn:module-score}
        \Delta s^{module}_{t,i,j}
    =
    s^{module}(z_{t+1,i,j}; C, C_{\text{type}})
    -
    s^{module}(z_t; C, C_{\text{type}}).
\end{equation}

where $J$, $P$, and $R$ denote Jaccard, precision, and recall respectively, the weights $\alpha, \beta, \gamma$ are set per task type. Recovery upweights recall ($\alpha,\beta,\gamma = 0.2, 0.2, 0.6$) to reward found members, refinement upweights precision ($0.2, 0.6, 0.2$) to penalize retained noise, and explanation weights them equally ($\tfrac{1}{3}$ each). This best-group formulation supports the multiple-groups state representation while keeping the hidden target single-module for scoring.

We score the mechanistic quality of a state as an evidence-grounded value in $[0,1]$, computed from the evidence the agent has actually retrieved. It combines grounding, meaning that the entities named in a claim appear in observed evidence, consensus between enrichment and annotation support, specificity, which penalizes generic claims, network support, stability across query subsets, and agreement across tools. A state that asserts a mechanism is scored on these components and heavily discounted when a claim is made without supporting evidence. A state that abstains is instead rewarded for withholding a claim under weak support, but this reward is forced to zero on any task other than \texttt{none}, so that abstention cannot be used to collect reward where a grounded group was recoverable. The per-step mechanistic reward is the change in this value between consecutive states, and is zero when no mechanistic claim has been made.

$p_{eff}$ is a small penalty designed to discourage two behaviors: excessively long trajectories and invalid tool calling. Long trajectories are penalized via the term $t / T_{\max}$, while we count invalid tool calls (those that are schema-invalid, exact duplicates, or return empty observations) with $n_{inv}$, penalizing them proportionally to the total number of tool calls: $(n_{inv} / n_{total})$. We formalize this scoring term as
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:eff-pen}
    p^{eff}_{t,i,j} = \lambda_1 (t / T_{\max}) + \lambda_2(n_{inv} / n_{total}).
\end{equation}

For the \texttt{none} task type, where the hidden target is empty, the membership component is replaced by an abstention score
\[
    s^{none} = w_{rel}\, \mathbf{1}[rel = \texttt{insufficient\_support}] + w_{abs}\, \mathbf{1}[G = \varnothing],
\]

with $w_{rel} = 0.6$ and $w_{abs} = 0.4$. This rewards the agent both for declaring no shared mechanism and for refusing to emit a spurious group.

\subsubsection{Terminal Reward Decomposition for GRPO}\label{sec:terminal-reward}
Unlike DPO, which compares local same-prefix branches, policy gradient methods require a scalar reward for a completed rollout. We therefore define a terminal reward over the final structured state and trajectory log:

\begin{equation}\label{eqn:terminal-reward}
    R_T = w^T_s s^T_{schema} +w^T_{abs} s^{T}_{module} + w^T_{c} \Delta s^{T}_{module} + w^T_{mabs} s^T_{mech} + w^T_{m} \Delta s^{T}_{mech} - w^T_e p^{T}_{eff}. 
\end{equation}

While all terms included in local scoring appear in the terminal reward, they are modified to capture global behavior. Additionally, two new rewards are included to capture absolute recovery. Absolute terms appear in the terminal score but not the local score. Because local scoring is evaluated on examples that share the same starting state $S_t$, absolute levels cancel, and only relative metrics matter. Conversely, the terminal reward is evaluating complete trajectories, which may vary in starting difficulty and length, so absolute metrics capture global problem-solving ability.

The terminal module score applies the same composite of Jaccard, precision, and recall to the final predicted group against the hidden target, and the cumulative module delta is its change from the group the agent was given at initialization. The terminal mechanistic terms mirror this, using the final evidence-grounded quality and its change over the trajectory, with both set to zero when no mechanistic claim has been made. The terminal schema score additionally requires that the termination mode, whether the agent stopped on its own or exhausted its budget, is recorded in the final state.

% ===== LIFTED main.tex:546-549 -- terminal efficiency penalty + Eq 4. ====
Finally, the terminal efficiency penalty aggregates trajectory length and invalid tool use across all steps:
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:terminal-eff}
    p^{T}_{eff} = \lambda_1 \left(\frac{T}{T_{\max}}\right) + \lambda_2 \left(\frac{\sum_{t=0}^{T-1} n^{t}_{inv}}{\sum_{t=0}^{T-1} n^{t}_{total}}\right).
\end{equation}

At each decision point we form DPO preference tuples from the candidate pool, pairing the shared-prefix context with one preferred and one dispreferred continuation. Each continuation carries the reasoning step, the tool action, the verifier update, and the continuation decision produced in Algorithm~\ref{alg:ch4-trajectory-gen}. Scores are min-max normalized within each pool first, so that the selection margin means the same thing across task types and trajectory lengths. \comment{figure for pref. construction}

The preferred candidate is the branch selected as the trajectory continuation rather than the strict score maximizer, since selection resolves near-ties using task-specific heuristics and therefore reflects the move judged best for the task. Candidates within a margin $\tau$ of the selected branch are discarded so that no pair is built from a near tie, and the remainder form the dispreferred pool. From that pool we draw examples across \texttt{easy}, \texttt{medium}, and \texttt{hard} difficulty bins, taking the lowest, median, and highest scoring remaining candidates respectively. Each pair is stored with its bin, task type, decision step, scores, and margin, and the corpus is rebalanced across task types and bins so that training is not dominated by the categories that happen to yield the most informative pairs.

Each retained trajectory is also logged as a sequence of turn records, from which we render short per-step findings and a final mechanistic summary. These become supervised examples and preference pairs for the training pipeline described in Section~\ref{sec:ch4-training-pipeline}. The local scores above supply the preference signal for DPO, while the terminal reward supplies the scalar return optimized during GRPO.

We will employ a training curriculum throughout the optimization process, scaling problem difficulty with model performance. 

During the SFT stage, we scale difficulty by moving from closed-book biological questions to open-book biological questions that require tool use. This promotes learning biological understanding first, and then moving to structured, schema-respecting tool calling tasks second.

After training a joint closed- and open-book SFT checkpoint, we scale difficulty for DPO pairs by training on increasingly difficult examples, based on difficulty bins defined in Section~\ref{sec:ch4-reward}. We begin DPO training by limiting preference pairs to $d = \texttt{easy}$ examples, then adding $\texttt{medium}$ and $\texttt{hard}$ examples as training saturates for each difficulty. After DPO training converges on $\texttt{hard}$ preference pairs, we transition to online RL to promote exploration beyond the behaviors captured in logged trajectory outputs.

We scale long-horizon reinforcement learning difficulty with several strategies. First, we modulate the trajectory length $T_{\max}$, starting with shorter exploration budgets and gradually increasing exploration length for problems that require multi-hop exploration. Additionally, we modulate realized module size, beginning training on small modules and shifting to larger ones as rewards saturate. Finally, we scale the per-task difficulty level, which jointly controls the number of dropped and added genes and the source-specific distractor band from which noise and \texttt{none} genes are drawn: dendrogram-distance bands for \textsc{Mentor}-derived modules (Section~\ref{sec:ch4-corpora}) and post-elbow rank bands for RWR-LOE modules (Section~\ref{sec:ch4-corpora}). Higher difficulties remove or add more genes and draw distractors from nearer, more easily confused regions of the corresponding module source, making recovery and refinement harder. At each stage, training batches are sampled from both the \textsc{Mentor}-derived and RWR-LOE module pools and are rebalanced by task type and difficulty bin to prevent either corpus from dominating training.


\subsection{Evaluation}
We evaluate the model during both stages of training to ensure that both stages are yielding improvements in mechanistic reasoning. We discuss the evaluation for stage 1 (SFT) in Section~\ref{sec:ch4-stage1-eval} and the evaluation for stage 2 (DPO and GRPO) in Section~\ref{sec:ch4-stage2-eval}.

\subsubsection{Stage 1 Evaluation: World Model SFT}\label{sec:ch4-stage1-eval}
At each step throughout the stage 1 curriculum, we evaluate the model using a step-specific gate that measures the model's ability to retrieve relevant evidence, internalize necessary context in parameters, and produce valid tool calls. We evaluate the model on a held-out set of closed-book and open-book questions, which depend on the curriculum stage. Each gate is specified in Table~\ref{tab:ch4-curriculum}. Passing each step's gate indicates that the model has learned the necessary skills to progress to the next step in the curriculum, so we use these gates as both a performance measurement at each stage and a progression pipeline for model training. Additionally, we define three flagship evaluation tasks that measure the model's overall ability to perform mechanistic reasoning after this stage: held-out context hierarchy generation, projection and return to network, and probing and intervention.

Held-out context hierarchy generation entails training on single contexts and/or combinations of contexts, and then requiring the model to produce the correct context-specific hierarchy for a lattice point that was never seen in training. Given a held-out context's active layer descriptor and a query gene set, the model returns co-membership for gene pairs, LCA depth per pair, and a predicted partition of the query set. We use the runtime dendrogram of the held-out context for scoring, measuring the adjusted Rand index, same-clade accuracy, and LCA depth accuracy of the model's predictions. Because hierarchies diverge across contexts, the model must utilize the operators learned in stage 1 to build context specific structure, rather than memorizing a single hierarchy.  

Projection and return to network evaluation utilizes the projection operation defined in Section~\ref{sec:ch4-task-cases} to measure the model's ability to project a module onto its enriched clade on a context. Given an input module or seed, we require the model to return the significantly enriched clade(s) for that module, the connection paths between those clades, and the mechanistic evidence that supports those connections. We score the model on the Jaccard accuracy of the predicted against the true clades and of the predicted against the true connection paths, as well as the mechanistic quality of the evidence returned. Scoring for all three flagships is summarized in Table~\ref{tab:ch4-flagship-scoring}. This evaluation measures the model's ability to reason over the multiplex structure and recover mechanistic connections between distant clades, unifying the alternative views of the multiplex into a single mechanistic interpretation.

Finally, to ensure that the model has truly internalized the lattice operators in its parameters, we will perform a probing and intervention evaluation. We will train probes in the form of multi-layer perceptron classifiers on the model's activations to recover, edge presence, layer membership, clade co-membership, and connector status. High classification accuracy indicates that the structure is recoverable from the model's internal representations, but decodability alone is not sufficient, since a probe can read structure that the model never uses. Then, we will perform interventions by modulating the model's activations to flip the predicted value of a probe, and measure the causal effect of this perturbation. If flipping a probed value changes the model's answer, the recovered structure is causal for its reasoning rather than decorative. We therefore treat probing as a gate rather than a diagnostic: no stage advances on accuracy alone if the probe test shows that the structure the model appears to hold is not the structure it actually uses. We additionally run these probes under the lean context condition, without the full rank-vector legend, since a model that reads a vector correctly only when the scaffold is present has not yet moved the operator into its weights.

\subsubsection{Stage 2 Evaluation: Mechanistic Agent DPO and GRPO}\label{sec:ch4-stage2-eval}
We will use several metrics to evaluate the effectiveness of our model. Each metric will be calculated by parsing the model's terminal structured state $S_T$, allowing for scalable evaluation of our checkpoints. All evaluations will be conducted on a held out set of \textsc{Mentor-EV}- and RWR-LOE-derived modules from the brain multiplex. We measure the model's ability to recover modules and cross-clade paths with Jaccard, precision, recall, and F1. Jaccard penalizes both missing and extra genes, while precision and recall separate the two error directions. F1 gives a balanced summary of precision and recall. We additionally report mechanistic grounding quality to test whether claims align with the evidence retrieved by the model.

We will report the weighted composite reward $R_T$, defined in Section~\ref{sec:ch4-reward}, to directly compare training reward magnitude on validation and holdout splits. If the task type is \texttt{none}, meaning $C = \varnothing$, we will parse the $S_T$ for the predicted relationship $rel_T$. Along with computing global accuracy, we will distinguish between both error directions, analyzing when the model claims no mechanism exists for truly related gene sets and when the model fabricates a mechanism for truly unrelated entities. Furthermore, we will report efficiency metrics, such as mean trajectory length, budget utilization, and invalid tool call rate. This allows for comparison between holdout efficiency and validation efficiency metrics computed in Equation~\ref{eqn:terminal-eff}.

In addition to general evaluation, we will stratify our evaluation across several axes. For module-level tasks, we measure evaluation across  module size, noise, task type, and module source. Additionally, we stratify across lattice tier and cut-height granularity to measure performance in different regions of the lattice. Reporting stratified metrics allows us to analyze how \textsc{Mentor-RL} performs as task difficulty increases and task type changes, and comparing metrics by module source exposes whether the model generalizes across module construction methods or exhibits biases toward one of the two module types.

In conjunction with empirical validation, which measures model accuracy, we will conduct blind comparative evaluations to measure expert domain scientists' model preferences. First, we will perform an inter-model preference test, supplying interpretation outputs from \textsc{Mentor-RL}, GPT-5~\cite{gpt5}, gpt-oss-120b~\cite{gpt-oss}, LLaMA 4~\cite{llama4}, Kimi K2~\cite{kimik2}, and Nemotron 3 Super~\cite{nemotron3}. We will then ask scientists to indicate their preferred interpretation, and with this information, we will calculate the proportion of \textsc{Mentor-RL} outputs that are preferred compared to other models. To support interpretation of these proportions, we will overlap a subset of evaluation pairs across raters and report inter-expert rating reliability.

After conducting inter-model preference testing, we will perform intra-model preference testing to compare preferences for different model checkpoints. Using the same protocol described for inter-model comparative evaluation, we will compare the base, SFT, DPO, and GRPO-trained \textsc{Mentor-RL} checkpoints. Blind comparative testing will allow us to evaluate model characteristics such as style, tone, and concision that are not easily evaluable with empirical metrics.

\subsubsection{Ablation Studies}
To ensure that each component of our methodology contributes to an improvement in mechanistic interpretation, we will ablate both training stages and design elements. All ablations will be evaluated using the holdout metrics defined in Section~\ref{sec:ch4-stage2-eval}. 

We will ablate phases of our training pipeline to confirm that each stage improves over the previous checkpoints. First, we will test our SFT-only checkpoint compared to the SFT- and DPO-trained checkpoint to ensure that preference optimization yields interpretation gains. Next, we will compare the DPO checkpoint to the full training pipeline to determine whether long-horizon RL training leads to improvement over local preference training. Finally, we will examine the effects of training with only closed-book SFT data vs. a mixed closed- and open-book training set to evaluate whether tool-calling examples in SFT lead to better DPO optimization. \comment{last sentence is no longer relevant?}

Along with testing various training stages, we will ablate architectural and reward decisions. First, we will ablate the policy's verifier mode, removing it to test whether self-verification is useful for producing better results. Additionally, we will remove the efficiency penalty $p^{eff}$ from the reward to test whether \textsc{Mentor-RL} can learn reasonable trajectory lengths without regularization. Finally, we will ablate difficulty bins in our training curriculum, training on all difficulty bins simultaneously rather than staging them progressively. This ablation will allow us to examine whether the model can learn from hard examples without curriculum staging.

Finally, we ablate two decisions about how graph content is presented to the model during Stage 1. The first is the rank-vector rendering. Because a rank vector is a deterministic view of one runtime output, the same vector can be shown in several ways, and we compare three: a structured table giving every entry a rank, a band, and a copied score; a pure ordinal sequence of gene identifiers with only the elbow marked; and a hybrid that keeps the ordering and the elbow while expressing magnitude through bands and calibration anchors rather than per-entry values. We compare these on the S4 interpretation, comparison, and recovery families under the probe condition, holding field order constant and drawing any exemplars from the training split, and fix the winning rendering before the full build. The second is the context fade schedule, in which the legend, calibration anchors, and linked hierarchy views supplied alongside each example are thinned as the curriculum advances. We test whether accuracy holds as the scaffold is withdrawn, since a model that reads a rank vector correctly only when the full legend is present has not internalized the operator and would not survive the leaner condition the deployed agent actually runs under.

\section{Expected Results}
After training across both stages, we expect \textsc{Mentor-RL} to recover modules, cross-module paths, and mechanistic evidence with high accuracy on held-out lattice contexts. Since these contexts are unseen during training, are expected results should indicate generalization to out-of-distribution data. Additionally, we expect the model to improve across Stage 1 and Stage 2 checkpoints, providing us with trained SFT and RL checkpoints for future use and application.

\subsection{Expected Empirical Results}
Empirically, we expect \textsc{Mentor-RL} to perform several tasks with high accuracy. First, we expect the model to recover modules and cross-module paths with Jaccard, precision, recall, and F1 scores at a rate higher than random chance. These results would indicate that the model has learned true lattice structure and exploration in training. Additionally, we expect the model to perform well on projection tasks, returning the correct enriched clades and connector paths for a given module, once again at a rate higher than random chance. We additionally expect our probes to decode structure from the model's activations with high accuracy, and interventions to show that the decoded structure is causal for the model's reasoning.

\subsection{Expected Behavioral Results}
Beyond accuracy, we expect three behavioral outcomes, each paired with an ablation in Section~\ref{sec:ch4-stage2-eval} that would falsify it. First, the act-verify loop should reduce schema-invalid tool calls and unsupported mechanistic claims relative to a verifier-ablated baseline, measured by invalid tool call rate and ungrounded-claim rate on the holdout split. Second, the efficiency penalty should produce shorter average trajectories without loss of recovery accuracy relative to a penalty-ablated baseline. Third, blind comparative evaluation should show that expert scientists prefer \textsc{Mentor-RL} outputs to base-policy outputs at a significantly higher rate, with preference rising across the base, SFT, DPO, and GRPO checkpoints and improving relative to frontier models across successive training stages.

\subsection{Deliverables}
We will release, under licenses compatible with the underlying data sources, the following artifacts:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item the lattice construction code and context ontology,
    \item the lattice-grounded training environment, including the \texttt{ctypes} multiplex runtime, tool vocabulary, and deterministic scoring functions,
    \item train, validation, and test split indices at the context, subtree, and tuple level, a leakage audit, and the code required to reconstruct the raw training corpus and cached API responses,
    \item trained \textsc{Mentor-RL} checkpoints for each stage of the pipeline (SFT stages S0-S9, DPO, and GRPO),
    \item the flagship harnesses, including held-out contexts and reserved target genes, and 
    \item training and generation code, curriculum configurations, and logged trajectories to support reproducible re-training and ablation.
\end{enumerate}

By releasing a deterministically-scored, open-source mechanistic exploration world model and agent together with its training environment, we aim to lower the cost of biological interpretation research and establish a reproducible benchmark that does not require proprietary model access. The scoring framework is domain-specific, but the training process to learn operators over networks in a lattice structure is transferable to other scientific settings with curated ground truth.

\section{Discussion}
\subsection{Preliminary Results}
We have achieved several preliminary results that support the feasibility of our proposed approach. First, we have successfully demonstrated full-fine tuning of a 120 billion parameter open-weight model, gpt-oss-120b~\cite{gpt-oss}, using data, tensor, and expert parallelism on OLCF's Frontier supercomputer. We have demonstrated training capability on up to 128 nodes. This infrastructure, as well as the ability to test smaller models such as gpt-oss-20b under the same training and evaluation pipeline, allows us to meet the model capacity requirements for training on the massive multiplex lattice dataset. \comment{add training curves} 

Additionally, we have successfully implemented the trajectory generation loop for Stage 2~\ref{sec:ch4-agent-loop} using the same gpt-oss-120b model. The trajectory generation loop runs end-to-end, produces a dashboard for inspecting runs, and achieves 0 parsing errors during runtime. These preliminary results demonstrate that the proposed agentic training loop is feasible without errors. However, it is important to note that initializing the trajectory generation loop using a base model without Stage 1 training leads to poor performance. Although the protocol achieves success on refinement tasks, it fails on recovery tasks and provides subpar mechanistic grounding. Therefore, these preliminary results support the need for Stage 1 training to provide the model with sufficient domain knowledge and tool-calling capabilities before Stage 2 training. Additionally, we note that these preliminary results are based on a small number of runs and do not include the full evaluation suite. We will conduct a more thorough evaluation of the trajectory generation loop after Stage 1 training is complete.

Finally, we have successfully generated the corpora for Stage 1 S0 and S1 curriculum steps. Our current focus is S0 training, which consists of ensembl-to-symbol and symbol-to-ensembl mapping tasks, along with ambiguous symbol resolution tasks. 

To begin Stage 1 training, we are studying the effect of tokenization on the model's ability to perform mapping tasks, testing four different approaches. First, we use the gpt-oss-120b base tokenizer as a control, which has been fit to the gpt-oss-120b pretraining corpus with byte-pair encoding (BPE). Second, we use a BPE tokenizer to fit only ensembl identifiers and gene symbols, and concatenate the resulting vocabulary with the gpt-oss-120b base tokenizer; we call this approach \textbf{domain-bpe} tokenization. Third, we encode the ensembl identifier prefix (ENSG) as an atomic token, and then fit a BPE tokenizer to the remaining identifier suffixes and gene symbols, concatenating this vocabulary with the base vocabulary once again. We term this approach \textbf{atomic+domain-bpe} tokenization. Finally, we assign each unique identifier and gene symbol an atomic token, and concatenate this vocabulary with the base vocabulary; we call this approach \textbf{atomic} tokenization. 

Our preliminary results show that base tokenization performs poorly on mapping tasks. We trained a gpt-oss-120b model on a small subset of the S0 corpus using each of the four tokenization approaches, with the model training on approximately 2,000 mapping questions and being evaluated on a set of 1,000 mapping questions. We found that the base tokenizer achieved an accuracy of 45.6\% on mapping tasks, while atomic tokenization performed best, achieving an accuracy of 85.9\%. However, these results are preliminary and based on a small subset of the S0 corpus. We will apply these tokenization approaches at scale to the full S0 corpus and evaluate their performance to determine the best approach for Stage 1 training. \comment{add preliminary performance table}

\subsection{Limitations and Future Work}
We identify several potential limitations and extensions to our proposed approach. First, the lattice strucutre operates on the assumption that different contexts generate genuinely different hierarchies. We plan to validate this assumption by comparing the hierarchies generated from different contexts, but it is possible that contexts may not be meaningfully different, which would limit the utility of the lattice structure. Additionally, our approach requires extensive compute resources to both construct the corpus with RWR-LOE and \textsc{Mentor-EV} and to train the model on the multiplex lattice dataset. While we have access to OLCF's Frontier supercomputer, this may limit the accessibility of our approach to other researchers. 

There are several avenues to extend this work in the future. First, we can extend the lattice to include additional contexts, such as other organ systems or disease states, providing a more comprehensive and general model checkpoint. Additionally, we can extend the lattice view across species, allowing for cross-species mechanistic reasoning. Finally, we can extend the evidence modes and curriculum stages, allowing for more operators to be learned and more complex mechanistic reasoning to be performed across several modalities.
